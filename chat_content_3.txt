TL;DR: The research is trying to solve one simple problem:

We have thousands of knee MRI scans, but we do not have trustworthy expert labels for most of them. Instead, we mostly have the radiologist’s written report. How can we use those reports to teach an AI model without blindly assuming that every report is perfectly correct and complete?

That is the core of the project. deep-research-report

1. First, forget about the AI model

Think of the dataset like this.

For one patient, we have:

Knee MRI images

and something like a radiologist's report:

“Possible partial ACL tear. Small joint effusion. No fracture.”

Ideally, to train an AI system, we would want an expert to look at every MRI and give us clean labels:

ACL abnormality → Yes

MCL abnormality → No

Fracture → No

Effusion → Yes

etc.

There are 12 abnormalities in total.

The problem is that doing this manually for thousands of MRI studies is expensive and time-consuming.

In this dataset, the research report found that the public training data appears to contain about 4,407 MRI studies but only 58 studies with direct expert challenge labels. Most of the other studies instead have their original radiology reports. deep-research-report

So the natural idea is:

“Can we read the reports and automatically create labels from them?”

Yes—but that creates the actual research problem.

2. Why can't we simply use the report as the correct answer?

Because a radiology report is not a perfect checklist of all 12 abnormalities.

Suppose the report says:

“ACL tear.”

That clearly tells us:

ACL = positive.

Easy.

But suppose it says:

“ACL is intact.”

That means:

ACL = negative.

Also easy.

Now suppose it says:

“Possible partial ACL tear.”

That is not definitely positive or definitely negative.

It means:

ACL = uncertain.

And suppose the report says absolutely nothing about the ACL.

What does that mean?

It could mean:

“The ACL is normal.”

But it could also mean:

“The radiologist simply didn't think it was important enough to mention.”

Those are completely different things.

The research report identifies this as one of the most important weaknesses in naïvely converting reports into labels. deep-research-report

3. This gives us four possible states

Instead of forcing every report statement immediately into:

Yes / No

our research proposes representing each abnormality as:

1. Positive

The report clearly says the abnormality exists.

Example:

“ACL tear is present.”

2. Explicitly negative

The report clearly says it does not exist.

Example:

“ACL is intact.”

3. Uncertain

The radiologist isn't sure.

Example:

“Possible partial ACL tear.”

4. Unmentioned

The report says nothing about it.

This fourth category is especially important.

The project asks:

Should “not mentioned” really be treated as “No”?

That sounds like a small detail, but it may affect thousands of training labels.

4. What are current simple approaches doing?

A simple approach might convert the report into:

ACL mentioned as abnormal → 1
Otherwise → 0

So if ACL isn't mentioned:

ACL = 0

The AI then learns from these values as if they were correct.

We call these automatically produced labels pseudo-labels or weak labels.

They're called "weak" because they were not directly assigned by an expert specifically for our classification task.

5. What is our research proposing instead?

Instead of saying:

“This label is definitely 0 or definitely 1,”

we want to say:

“Based on what the report says and how reliable this kind of statement usually is, how likely is the true answer to be positive?”

For example, imagine our analysis discovers:

If report explicitly says ACL tear:
91% of expert labels agree.

If report says ACL is normal:
96% agree.

If report is uncertain:
55% actually turn out positive.

If ACL isn't mentioned:
15% still turn out positive.

These numbers are just examples, not actual results.

Instead of training with:

Positive   → 1
Negative   → 0
Uncertain  → 0
Unmentioned → 0

we might train with something like:

Positive    → 0.91
Negative    → 0.04
Uncertain   → 0.55
Unmentioned → 0.15

Now the AI receives information about uncertainty, rather than being taught that uncertain information is perfectly certain.

That is the central research idea.

6. But how do we know which report statements are trustworthy?

This is where the small set of expert-labeled MRI studies becomes extremely useful.

For those cases we have both:

What the original report said

and

What the challenge's expert reviewers later decided from the MRI.

So we can compare them.

For example:

Report: "Possible ACL tear"
Expert label: ACL abnormality = Yes

Another:

Report: ACL not mentioned
Expert label: ACL abnormality = Yes

Another:

Report: "No fracture"
Expert label: Fracture = No

Across these examples we can measure:

How trustworthy is each kind of report statement for each abnormality?

The attached research report specifically highlights the important point that the original clinical report and the challenge reference labels come from different interpretations of the MRI, so they should not automatically be treated as identical ground truth. deep-research-report

7. Where does the AI model come into this?

Once we have created better training labels, we train an image model.

You don't need to think deeply about the internal neural network yet.

Conceptually, it is just a function:

Knee MRI
    ↓
AI
    ↓
Probability of each of 12 abnormalities

For example:

ACL abnormality       87%
MCL abnormality       11%
Medial meniscus       73%
Lateral meniscus      14%
Fracture               3%
Effusion              81%
...

The important point is:

The main research is not inventing a fancy MRI model.

In fact, we deliberately want to keep the image model relatively standard.

Why?

Because we want to test whether changing the quality of the training labels is what improves the results.

If we also completely change the image model, we won't know what caused the improvement.

8. The experiment is therefore very simple conceptually

We train the same MRI model twice.

Model A — Normal approach

Train it using hard report labels:

Yes = 1
Everything else = 0

or another conventional hard-label baseline.

Model B — Our approach

Train exactly the same model using our uncertainty-aware labels.

For example:

positive → high probability
negative → low probability

17. The whole FYP in one paragraph

We have thousands of knee MRI scans, but expert labels are scarce. Most scans instead have a radiologist's written report. Those reports contain useful information, but they are incomplete and uncertain: a doctor may explicitly identify a condition, explicitly reject it, express uncertainty, or simply not mention it. Instead of converting every report into hard Yes/No labels and treating those labels as perfect truth, our research will estimate how reliable different types of report statements are using the small expert-labeled subset. We will then generate uncertainty-aware training labels, train an MRI model using them, and compare it against the conventional hard-label approach. Finally, the research will be built into a complete system that accepts real knee MRI DICOM studies, supports research and error analysis, and can analyze a new MRI using images alone.

The one sentence you should remember for explaining this to your supervisor or panel

We are researching whether an MRI model can learn better from radiology reports if we explicitly account for the fact that the reports are incomplete and uncertain instead of treating every report-derived label as perfect ground truth.

1. First: what other research paths are available?

Beyond KneeWS, the report gives several distinct directions.

One is reports as privileged information: instead of converting the radiology report into 12 labels, use the complete report to teach the MRI model during training through image-text learning or teacher-student distillation. The research report considers this high-novelty, high-depth, feasible with a consumer GPU if representations are cached, and potentially publishable. RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection…

9. The complete KneeSet system

I would give it nine major capabilities.

DICOM Study Ingestion and Reconstruction — Foundation engineering. The system accepts an actual knee MRI examination, reconstructs its study → series → slices hierarchy, validates the files, orders slices correctly, and extracts relevant acquisition metadata. This is essential because the research problem exists specifically at the series level, not in pre-exported JPG images.

MRI Series Characterization — Supporting AI / domain processing. For every series, the system determines robust properties such as anatomical orientation—sagittal, coronal, axial—and, where sufficiently reliable, sequence characteristics such as fluid-sensitive or other acquisition families. The research report specifically recommends using DICOM metadata and geometry rather than trusting SeriesDescription alone. deep-research-report

Variable-Series Study Representation — Core research. Instead of forcing every examination into three predetermined slots, KneeSet creates a representation from whatever usable series are present. Each series is encoded separately and then combined through a permutation-invariant aggregation mechanism such as mean pooling, attention pooling or a small set model. This is the core structural difference from the baseline.

Series-Type-Aware Fusion — Core research. The system does not treat every series as interchangeable. It attaches information such as orientation/sequence type to the image representation so that the model can distinguish, conceptually, “this evidence came from a sagittal series” from “this evidence came from an axial series.” The report explicitly proposes plane and sequence-type embeddings as part of this design. RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection…

Missing-Series Robustness Training — Core research. During training, one or more MRI series are randomly removed. The model must still make predictions from the remaining evidence. This is analogous to intentionally training a system under partial sensor failure so it does not become dependent on one exact input configuration.

Twelve-Abnormality MRI Analysis — Supporting AI. The aggregated study representation produces probabilities for the same 12 RSNA abnormalities. The purpose of this feature is to give the robustness research a meaningful downstream medical-imaging task; the 12-label classifier itself is not the novelty.

Protocol Stress-Testing Engine — Core research/evaluation. The system can deliberately simulate degraded examinations: remove one preferred series, remove all series from one plane, keep only one or two planes, randomly remove a fraction of series, or vary the number of usable inputs. It then measures exactly how performance degrades.

Series Contribution and Evidence Explorer — User-facing analytical capability. For an individual study, the researcher can inspect which series were available, how the system classified them, which were used, and how the prediction changes when one is removed. This makes the robustness question visible rather than hiding everything behind an aggregate AUC.

Experiment, Evaluation and Deployable Inference Workspace — Engineering/analytical capability. The system stores model versions, experiment configuration, preprocessing settings, baseline versus proposed results, robustness curves, compute cost and inference history, while also providing a deployable mode where an operator can supply a new DICOM study and receive 12 predictions regardless of whether the study contains the “ideal” series configuration.

30. Why this could be an especially good FYP

KneeSet has several advantages over KneeWS.

It is much more visibly a medical-imaging/computer-vision system.

The complexity is easy to demonstrate:

“MRI studies don't all look structurally identical.”

The end product is visually strong.

You can show the actual study structure, series, slices, missing input and performance degradation.

It also avoids making the FYP heavily dependent on:

multilingual LLMs;

prompt engineering;

the statistical adequacy of only 58 gold labels for calibrating report semantics.

And the research report itself describes this as the strongest imaging-centric project, with high novelty, high technical difficulty, good feasibility and strong publication potential. RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection…

31. Where KneeSet is weaker than KneeWS

There are important disadvantages.

The largest one is engineering risk.

KneeWS can start doing useful weak-label experiments before the entire MRI pipeline is mature.

KneeSet cannot.

Its research depends heavily on understanding the DICOM series correctly.

Another issue is that KneeSet needs the report-derived labels as upstream training supervision anyway, so you still need some stable label-generation approach—even though it is not your research contribution.

It is also more GPU-heavy.

And you have to be disciplined not to turn the project into:

“Try ten MRI architectures and report which one wins.”

The thesis must remain about protocol robustness.

32. KneeWS versus KneeSet

Conceptually, they investigate different failures:

KneeWS

MRI ← questionable labels ← report
                ↑
          research here

versus:

KneeSet

Variable MRI series → model → prediction
        ↑
   research here

KneeWS asks:

Can we trust the training information?

KneeSet asks:

Can we trust the model when the available imaging information changes?

I would currently characterize them this way:

KneeWS: lower implementation risk, cleaner research experiment, stronger weak-supervision/data-science identity.

KneeSet: higher implementation risk, stronger CV/medical-imaging identity, stronger visual demonstration, probably stronger system complexity.

Both can satisfy FAST's central requirement that a research-derived FYP must end in an operable, evaluated, deployable system rather than only a notebook. FAST_FYP_Student_Idea_Selection…

33. Registration-ready definition

Title: A Protocol-Robust Multi-Series System for Multi-Label Knee MRI Analysis

Problem: Existing knee MRI models commonly rely on a predefined set of imaging series, while real clinical MRI examinations may contain heterogeneous and partially missing acquisition protocols. Such fixed-input assumptions can cause unpredictable degradation when expected evidence is unavailable.

Research objective: Determine whether representing a knee MRI examination as a typed variable set of series and explicitly training under missing-series conditions improves robustness compared with fixed canonical-series models.

Research contribution: A protocol-aware variable-series representation and missing-series training strategy, evaluated using controlled MRI evidence removal, pathology-specific robustness analysis and standard multi-label classification metrics.

System objective: Build a deployable research platform capable of ingesting heterogeneous DICOM knee MRI studies, characterizing available acquisitions, performing variable-series abnormality analysis, simulating missing-protocol conditions and quantifying model robustness.

Central CCP: The system must produce stable multi-label MRI predictions from heterogeneous variable-size examinations while balancing evidence coverage, computational cost and dependence on individual MRI acquisitions, with no predetermined optimal strategy for selecting or combining series.

34. My current assessment

I would keep both KneeWS and KneeSet alive as serious registration candidates.

They optimize for different kinds of FYP.

If your priority is:

cleanest research methodology + lowest risk of experimental failure

KneeWS is stronger.

If your priority is:

medical imaging / computer vision depth + stronger visual system + more obvious engineering complexity

KneeSet may actually be more attractive.

The next most worthwhile system to develop after this one would be the privileged multimodal learning system, because that would give you a third fundamentally different choice:

KneeWS=How should reports become supervision?
KneeSet=How should variable MRI evidence be represented?
Multimodal system=Should reports become labels at all, or should the MRI model learn directly from the report semantics?

Those three form a strong, non-overlapping shortlist from the research report. RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection…
