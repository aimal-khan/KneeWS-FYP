# Role

Act as a senior AI/ML research scientist specializing in:

- medical imaging,
- MRI analysis,
- computer vision,
- multimodal vision-language learning,
- radiology NLP,
- weak/semi-supervised learning,
- clinical AI,
- and academic research methodology.

Approach this as an advisor helping an undergraduate Data Science student select a **research-oriented Final Year Project (FYP)** that could potentially lead to a technically strong thesis or research paper.

Do **not** approach this primarily as a Kaggle leaderboard optimization exercise.

---

# Context

I have to register my Final Year Project this semester and currently do not have a finalized research idea.

My supervisor suggested the following direction:

> Find a novel, complex, relatively new dataset and extract an interesting research problem from it rather than simply implementing an existing standard ML problem.

He specifically suggested exploring RSNA's datasets and AI challenges.

The challenge I am currently considering is:

**RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection**
[https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rsna-knee-abnormality-detection](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rsna-knee-abnormality-detection)

Also investigate the corresponding official RSNA challenge pages and documentation.

The central goal of this research is **not necessarily to solve exactly the Kaggle competition task**.

Instead, I want to determine:

1. what makes this dataset interesting,
2. what research questions become possible because of its characteristics,
3. what has already been researched,
4. where genuine gaps remain,
5. and which of those gaps could become a strong and feasible FYP.

---

# Primary Objective

Conduct a **deep research investigation of the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection challenge, its dataset, its surrounding research landscape, and possible research directions based on the dataset**.

At the end, I should understand the dataset well enough to discuss it intelligently with my supervisor and have a shortlist of **specific, defensible FYP research directions**.

Do not jump directly to proposing projects.

First understand the challenge and dataset deeply, then investigate prior literature, then derive possible research questions from the evidence.

---

# Phase 1 — Understand the Challenge Completely

Inspect all authoritative information available about the challenge, prioritizing:

1. official Kaggle competition pages,
2. official RSNA pages,
3. competition data descriptions,
4. competition rules/evaluation documentation,
5. official organizer material,
6. public Kaggle notebooks/discussions where useful,
7. repositories from serious participants where useful.

Determine precisely:

- What problem is RSNA trying to solve?
- What is the clinical motivation?
- Why was knee MRI selected?
- What are the expected model inputs?
- What are the expected outputs?
- Is this classification, detection, localization, segmentation, multimodal prediction, or some combination?
- Is it multi-class or multi-label?
- What abnormalities are being predicted?
- What is the exact prediction unit: patient, study, MRI series, slice, region, etc.?
- What evaluation metric is used?
- How is the train/test/evaluation setup constructed?
- What information exists during training versus inference?
- What makes this competition different from previous RSNA challenges?

Explain the challenge from both:

### Clinical perspective

What problem would a radiologist or healthcare system actually be trying to solve?

### Machine-learning perspective

What mathematical/ML problem is being presented?

---

# Phase 2 — Perform a Deep Dataset Investigation

Understand the dataset itself independently of the competition objective.

Investigate and document as much as can be verified about:

## Dataset size and structure

Determine:

- number of patients,
- number of MRI examinations/studies,
- number of MRI series,
- approximate number of images/slices,
- train/test split,
- labeled versus unlabeled/partially labeled data,
- metadata fields,
- patient demographics where available,
- institutions/sites represented,
- countries represented,
- languages represented in radiology reports.

If different official sources report different numbers, explicitly identify and investigate the discrepancy rather than silently choosing one.

## MRI data

Investigate:

- DICOM organization,
- study → series → slice hierarchy,
- imaging planes,
- sequence types,
- MRI protocols,
- resolution differences,
- number of series per study,
- variable slice counts,
- acquisition variability,
- scanner/vendor variability if known,
- site-specific differences.

Explain why these properties make modeling difficult.

## Radiology reports

Investigate:

- what report text is provided,
- which languages occur,
- whether reports are structured or free text,
- whether reports contain findings, impression, history, etc.,
- how heterogeneous the reports are,
- whether the reports themselves contain enough information to infer abnormality labels,
- how labels relate to the reports,
- whether NLP can potentially generate weak labels.

Pay special attention to the relationship between:

**MRI → radiology report → expert abnormality labels**

Understand exactly which information was produced by whom.

## Ground-truth labels

Determine:

- what labels are available,
- how many abnormalities exist,
- how labels were generated,
- which samples have expert labels,
- which samples do not,
- whether labels are incomplete,
- whether weak supervision is implicitly part of the challenge,
- whether label uncertainty exists,
- whether reports and expert labels can disagree.

Clearly distinguish facts documented by RSNA/Kaggle from participant interpretations.

## Dataset biases and limitations

Investigate potential:

- class imbalance,
- demographic bias,
- site bias,
- geographic bias,
- scanner/protocol bias,
- language bias,
- annotation bias,
- report-style bias,
- domain shift,
- label noise,
- missing-data issues,
- shortcut-learning opportunities,
- patient leakage or correlated samples.

Do not claim that a bias exists without evidence. Distinguish between:

- demonstrated issues,
- likely risks,
- and hypotheses worth studying.

---

# Phase 3 — Understand Why This Dataset Is Research-Interesting

Identify what makes this dataset unusual relative to standard medical-imaging datasets.

In particular, investigate the importance of the combination of:

- MRI,
- 3D/multi-series imaging,
- multiple anatomical planes,
- multiple abnormalities,
- free-text radiology reports,
- multilingual reports,
- international multi-center data,
- relatively sparse expert annotations,
- potentially noisy naturally occurring clinical data.

Determine which properties are actually novel and which are already common in the literature.

Ask:

> What scientific opportunities exist here specifically because this dataset has characteristics that older knee MRI datasets did not?

---

# Phase 4 — Conduct a Literature Review

Perform a targeted literature search covering the closest relevant research.

Prioritize:

- peer-reviewed journal papers,
- conference papers,
- PubMed,
- RSNA/Radiology publications,
- MICCAI,
- Medical Image Analysis,
- IEEE TMI,
- CVPR/ICCV/ECCV where relevant,
- NeurIPS/ICLR/AAAI where relevant,
- arXiv only when peer-reviewed work is unavailable or the work is very recent.

Include important historical work where necessary, but emphasize roughly the last 5 years for the state of the art.

Investigate at least these research areas:

### Knee MRI AI

Research involving:

- ACL injuries,
- meniscal tears,
- osteoarthritis,
- cartilage abnormalities,
- fractures,
- effusion,
- and other relevant knee abnormalities.

Investigate major previous datasets such as MRNet and other comparable knee MRI datasets.

Determine what problems have already been solved extensively.

---

### Multi-label knee MRI analysis

Investigate models that predict several knee abnormalities simultaneously.

Explore whether interactions or correlations between abnormalities have been modeled explicitly.

---

### 3D / multi-series / multi-view MRI learning

Investigate approaches for combining:

- sagittal,
- coronal,
- axial,
- different MRI sequences,
- multiple series,
- multiple slices.

Determine whether existing methods treat series independently or jointly.

---

### Multimodal radiology AI

Investigate research combining:

- medical images,
- radiology reports,
- structured labels.

Look at vision-language models and multimodal foundation models where relevant.

---

### Weak supervision from radiology reports

Investigate using reports to derive labels automatically.

Explore:

- rule-based extraction,
- clinical NLP,
- LLM-based label extraction,
- noisy-label learning,
- pseudo-labeling,
- weak supervision,
- teacher-student methods,
- positive-unlabeled learning.

Determine the established methods and unresolved limitations.

---

### Multilingual radiology NLP

Investigate:

- multilingual radiology reports,
- cross-lingual clinical NLP,
- machine translation versus multilingual encoders,
- label extraction across languages,
- representation alignment across languages.

Determine how mature this field is and whether this dataset introduces an unusual research opportunity.

---

### Semi-supervised and self-supervised medical imaging

Investigate methods that exploit large quantities of incompletely labeled medical images.

Include:

- self-supervised representation learning,
- contrastive learning,
- masked image modeling,
- pseudo-labeling,
- consistency regularization,
- multimodal pretraining.

---

### Multi-center generalization

Investigate:

- domain generalization,
- domain adaptation,
- scanner variability,
- institution-specific bias,
- cross-site validation,
- federated-style considerations where relevant.

Determine whether this dataset could support meaningful experiments on generalization.

---

### Model efficiency

Since clinical MRI studies can be large, investigate:

- computation-efficient MRI models,
- intelligent slice selection,
- series selection,
- 2D vs 2.5D vs 3D methods,
- efficient multimodal architectures,
- accuracy/compute trade-offs.

---

### Explainability and clinical reliability

Investigate relevant research involving:

- localization despite study-level labels,
- attention maps,
- saliency,
- uncertainty estimation,
- calibration,
- confidence-aware prediction,
- interpretability,
- failure detection.

Focus on scientifically meaningful evaluation rather than simply attaching Grad-CAM to a classifier.

---

# Phase 5 — Analyze Existing Work on This Specific Challenge

Because the competition is currently active, investigate available:

- Kaggle notebooks,
- Kaggle discussions,
- public GitHub repositories,
- baseline solutions,
- participant experiments,
- organizer-provided baselines.

Determine what competitors are already doing.

Look for common approaches such as:

- extracting labels from reports,
- translating reports,
- LLM-based report parsing,
- weak labeling,
- 2D CNNs,
- 2.5D methods,
- 3D networks,
- transformers,
- series selection,
- ensemble approaches,
- multimodal fusion,
- self-supervised models.

However, treat these sources as **engineering evidence rather than established scientific evidence** unless backed by peer-reviewed research.

This section is important because an FYP idea that sounds novel academically may already be trivial/common among active competition participants.

---

# Phase 6 — Identify Research Gaps

Based on the dataset characteristics + literature + current competition approaches, identify concrete research gaps.

Do not generate generic gaps such as:

- "use a transformer,"
- "use deep learning,"
- "improve accuracy,"
- "try an ensemble,"
- "use explainable AI,"
- "use an LLM."

A research gap should instead look like:

> Existing methods generally do X under assumption Y, but this dataset introduces condition Z. It is unclear whether A or B is more robust under Z. This can be investigated experimentally by...

For every gap explain:

1. What is already known?
2. What is missing or uncertain?
3. Why does this dataset allow the question to be investigated?
4. What hypothesis could be tested?
5. What experiment would test it?
6. What would constitute a meaningful contribution?
7. Is it actually novel enough for an undergraduate research project?

Distinguish clearly between:

- **methodological novelty**
- **empirical novelty**
- **dataset/benchmark analysis**
- **clinical/application novelty**

A project does not necessarily need a completely new neural-network architecture to constitute good research.

---

# Phase 7 — Generate Candidate FYP Research Directions

Only after completing the previous analysis, generate approximately **10–15 candidate research directions**.

Consider directions from multiple categories rather than making all of them model-architecture projects.

Potential categories to investigate include, but are not limited to:

- weak supervision,
- multimodal learning,
- multilingual report understanding,
- label quality,
- cross-site generalization,
- MRI series selection,
- multi-view fusion,
- representation learning,
- self-supervised learning,
- label-efficient learning,
- disease correlation modeling,
- calibration/uncertainty,
- robustness,
- interpretability/localization,
- efficient inference,
- dataset bias/auditing,
- image-report alignment,
- missing-modality robustness.

These are categories to investigate, **not predetermined project recommendations**.

If research suggests some of them are weak ideas, reject them.

For each proposed direction provide:

### Research question

A precise question the FYP would try to answer.

### Motivation

Why the problem matters.

### Gap

What existing research does not adequately answer.

### Hypothesis

A falsifiable hypothesis where possible.

### Proposed methodology

A realistic experimental design.

### Baseline

What it should be compared against.

### Evaluation

Appropriate metrics and experimental protocol.

### Contribution

What could actually be claimed if successful.

### Novelty

Rate as:

- Low
- Moderate
- High

and explain why.

### Difficulty

Rate as:

- Low
- Moderate
- High
- Very High

### Compute requirements

Estimate whether it is practical using:

- free Kaggle GPUs,
- Google Colab,
- one consumer GPU,
- or whether substantial compute would likely be required.

### FYP feasibility

Evaluate whether it can reasonably be completed by an undergraduate student within approximately one academic year.

### Publication potential

Estimate:

- weak,
- reasonable,
- strong,

with justification.

### Main risks

Explain what might cause the research idea to fail.

---

# Phase 8 — Rank the Best Ideas

After analyzing all candidate directions, shortlist the **best 5**.

Rank them based on:

1. research novelty,
2. scientific value,
3. fit with this particular dataset,
4. feasibility,
5. implementation complexity,
6. compute requirements,
7. ability to establish strong baselines,
8. availability of literature,
9. clarity of experimental evaluation,
10. likelihood of producing meaningful results even if the proposed method does not beat SOTA,
11. potential for an undergraduate thesis/publication.

Do not automatically rank the most technically complicated idea highest.

Prefer a **clear research question with strong experimental methodology** over an unnecessarily complicated architecture.

---

# Phase 9 — Develop the Top 3 in Detail

For the three strongest candidates, provide a preliminary research proposal.

For each include:

## Proposed title

A publication/thesis-style title.

## Problem statement

Precisely define the problem.

## Research question

One primary research question plus any useful secondary questions.

## Hypothesis

State what we expect to discover.

## Research contribution

Clearly state what would be new.

## Dataset usage

Explain exactly which components of the RSNA dataset would be used.

## Methodology

Describe the complete experimental methodology at a conceptual level.

Do not yet write implementation code.

## Baselines

Identify meaningful baselines from the literature.

## Ablation studies

Specify experiments necessary to understand *why* the proposed method works.

## Evaluation metrics

Specify appropriate metrics beyond simply the Kaggle leaderboard metric where scientifically useful.

## Experimental splits

Explain how train/validation/test or cross-validation should be constructed.

Pay special attention to avoiding:

- patient leakage,
- site leakage,
- pseudo-label leakage,
- report-derived target leakage.

## Expected outcomes

Explain what results would support or reject the hypothesis.

## Minimum viable research contribution

Describe what would still constitute a useful thesis result even if the proposed approach does not improve predictive performance.

## Risks and fallback scope

Describe how the FYP could be reduced in scope without becoming trivial.

---

# Phase 10 — Supervisor Discussion Preparation

At the end, provide a concise section titled:

# What I Should Discuss With My Supervisor

Give me the most important conclusions I should take into my next FYP meeting.

Include:

- why this dataset is worth considering,
- the most promising research gap,
- the top 3 project ideas,
- major feasibility concerns,
- data-access concerns,
- compute concerns,
- questions requiring medical/domain-expert guidance,
- and decisions that should be made before formally registering the project.

Also propose **5–10 technically informed questions** I should ask my supervisor.

For research papers provide:

- title,

---

## Recency

Because this is a **2026 competition**, search specifically for very recent material.

Clearly state the research cutoff date.

Older seminal literature should still be included where necessary.

---

## Fact vs inference

Explicitly distinguish:

- verified fact,
- interpretation,
- hypothesis,
- proposed research direction.

Do not infer undocumented properties of the dataset as facts.

---

## Conflicting information

If sources disagree about:

- dataset size,
- number of institutions,
- number of expert-labeled samples,
- label construction,
- train/test structure,
- or any other material property,

show the discrepancy and determine the most authoritative/current source if possible.

Do not silently reconcile inconsistent numbers.

---

## Data-access limitations

If raw competition files cannot be inspected because Kaggle authentication or competition enrollment is required:

1. say exactly what could not be inspected,
2. do not fabricate dataset statistics,
3. use official descriptions and reliable public analysis where possible,
4. distinguish verified metadata from third-party observations,
5. tell me which files I should provide later for a definitive local dataset audit.

---

# Important Research Constraint

Do not equate:

**"new model architecture" = "research."**

I am interested in research questions where the contribution may instead be:

- a systematic empirical finding,
- a robust evaluation methodology,
- a new use of multimodal information,
- an analysis of weak labels,
- an investigation of multilingual effects,
- an analysis of domain shift,
- a label-efficiency study,
- a robustness/generalization study,
- or another scientifically defensible contribution.

Conversely, do not recommend a project merely because it sounds sophisticated.

---

# Feasibility Constraint

This is an undergraduate Data Science FYP.

Avoid recommending projects whose meaningful execution would realistically require:

- a large research lab,
- dozens of high-end GPUs,
- manual annotation of thousands of MRIs by radiologists,
- access to confidential clinical datasets,
- or major medical expertise unavailable to a student.

When a promising idea has such dependencies, explicitly identify them.

Prefer projects where a strong experiment can be performed with the supplied RSNA data and realistic academic compute.

---

# Important Distinction: Kaggle vs Research

For every major idea, ask:

> Is this primarily a leaderboard technique, or does it answer a scientific question?

Reject ideas that are merely:

- hyperparameter tuning,
- larger backbones,
- brute-force ensembling,
- test-time augmentation,
- leaderboard-specific optimization,
- engineering tricks without a research hypothesis.

Kaggle performance may be used as an evaluation signal, but **the FYP must have a research question independent of leaderboard rank**.
