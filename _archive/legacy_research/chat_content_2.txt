TL;DR: The research is trying to solve one simple problem:

We have thousands of knee MRI scans, but we do not have trustworthy expert labels for most of them. Instead, we mostly have the radiologist’s written report. How can we use those reports to teach an AI model without blindly assuming that every report is perfectly correct and complete?

That is the core of the project. deep-research-report

1. First, forget about the AI model

Think of the dataset like this.

For one patient, we have:

Knee MRI images

and something like a radiologist's report:

“Possible partial ACL tear. Small joint effusion. No fracture.”

Ideally, to train an AI system, we would want an expert to look at every MRI and give us clean labels:

ACL abnormality → Yes

MCL abnormality → No

Fracture → No

Effusion → Yes

etc.

There are 12 abnormalities in total.

The problem is that doing this manually for thousands of MRI studies is expensive and time-consuming.

In this dataset, the research report found that the public training data appears to contain about 4,407 MRI studies but only 58 studies with direct expert challenge labels. Most of the other studies instead have their original radiology reports. deep-research-report

So the natural idea is:

“Can we read the reports and automatically create labels from them?”

Yes—but that creates the actual research problem.

2. Why can't we simply use the report as the correct answer?

Because a radiology report is not a perfect checklist of all 12 abnormalities.

Suppose the report says:

“ACL tear.”

That clearly tells us:

ACL = positive.

Easy.

But suppose it says:

“ACL is intact.”

That means:

ACL = negative.

Also easy.

Now suppose it says:

“Possible partial ACL tear.”

That is not definitely positive or definitely negative.

It means:

ACL = uncertain.

And suppose the report says absolutely nothing about the ACL.

What does that mean?

It could mean:

“The ACL is normal.”

But it could also mean:

“The radiologist simply didn't think it was important enough to mention.”

Those are completely different things.

The research report identifies this as one of the most important weaknesses in naïvely converting reports into labels. deep-research-report

3. This gives us four possible states

Instead of forcing every report statement immediately into:

Yes / No

our research proposes representing each abnormality as:

1. Positive

The report clearly says the abnormality exists.

Example:

“ACL tear is present.”

2. Explicitly negative

The report clearly says it does not exist.

Example:

“ACL is intact.”

3. Uncertain

The radiologist isn't sure.

Example:

“Possible partial ACL tear.”

4. Unmentioned

The report says nothing about it.

This fourth category is especially important.

The project asks:

Should “not mentioned” really be treated as “No”?

That sounds like a small detail, but it may affect thousands of training labels.

4. What are current simple approaches doing?

A simple approach might convert the report into:

ACL mentioned as abnormal → 1
Otherwise → 0

So if ACL isn't mentioned:

ACL = 0

The AI then learns from these values as if they were correct.

We call these automatically produced labels pseudo-labels or weak labels.

They're called "weak" because they were not directly assigned by an expert specifically for our classification task.

5. What is our research proposing instead?

Instead of saying:

“This label is definitely 0 or definitely 1,”

we want to say:

“Based on what the report says and how reliable this kind of statement usually is, how likely is the true answer to be positive?”

For example, imagine our analysis discovers:

If report explicitly says ACL tear:
91% of expert labels agree.

If report says ACL is normal:
96% agree.

If report is uncertain:
55% actually turn out positive.

If ACL isn't mentioned:
15% still turn out positive.

These numbers are just examples, not actual results.

Instead of training with:

Positive   → 1
Negative   → 0
Uncertain  → 0
Unmentioned → 0

we might train with something like:

Positive    → 0.91
Negative    → 0.04
Uncertain   → 0.55
Unmentioned → 0.15

Now the AI receives information about uncertainty, rather than being taught that uncertain information is perfectly certain.

That is the central research idea.

6. But how do we know which report statements are trustworthy?

This is where the small set of expert-labeled MRI studies becomes extremely useful.

For those cases we have both:

What the original report said

and

What the challenge's expert reviewers later decided from the MRI.

So we can compare them.

For example:

Report: "Possible ACL tear"
Expert label: ACL abnormality = Yes

Another:

Report: ACL not mentioned
Expert label: ACL abnormality = Yes

Another:

Report: "No fracture"
Expert label: Fracture = No

Across these examples we can measure:

How trustworthy is each kind of report statement for each abnormality?

The attached research report specifically highlights the important point that the original clinical report and the challenge reference labels come from different interpretations of the MRI, so they should not automatically be treated as identical ground truth. deep-research-report

7. Where does the AI model come into this?

Once we have created better training labels, we train an image model.

You don't need to think deeply about the internal neural network yet.

Conceptually, it is just a function:

Knee MRI
    ↓
AI
    ↓
Probability of each of 12 abnormalities

For example:

ACL abnormality       87%
MCL abnormality       11%
Medial meniscus       73%
Lateral meniscus      14%
Fracture               3%
Effusion              81%
...

The important point is:

The main research is not inventing a fancy MRI model.

In fact, we deliberately want to keep the image model relatively standard.

Why?

Because we want to test whether changing the quality of the training labels is what improves the results.

If we also completely change the image model, we won't know what caused the improvement.

8. The experiment is therefore very simple conceptually

We train the same MRI model twice.

Model A — Normal approach

Train it using hard report labels:

Yes = 1
Everything else = 0

or another conventional hard-label baseline.

Model B — Our approach

Train exactly the same model using our uncertainty-aware labels.

For example:

positive → high probability
negative → low probability

17. The whole FYP in one paragraph

We have thousands of knee MRI scans, but expert labels are scarce. Most scans instead have a radiologist's written report. Instead of converting every report into hard Yes/No labels and treating those labels as perfect truth, our research will estimate how reliable different types of report statements are using the small expert-labeled subset. Finally, the research will be built into a complete system that accepts real knee MRI DICOM studies, supports research and error analysis, and can analyze a new MRI using images alone.

Ambiguity

why is it called 'weakly' supervised system?

TL;DR: It is called weakly supervised because the MRI model is trained mostly using imperfect labels automatically inferred from radiology reports, rather than clean expert-created labels for every MRI study. deep-research-report

The word “weakly” does not mean the AI model itself is weak. It describes the quality/source of the supervision used during training.

In normal fully supervised learning, you would ideally have thousands of MRI studies where experts explicitly provide the correct 12 labels:

MRI 1 → ACL: Yes, MCL: No, Fracture: No, Effusion: Yes, ...

Then the model directly learns:

MRI→expert label

But in the RSNA dataset, according to the research report, the public training data contains roughly 4,407 studies but only 58 with direct expert challenge labels. For most of the remaining studies, what we have instead is the original radiologist's written report. deep-research-report

So for most studies, our pipeline looks like:

Radiology Report→automatically inferred label

and then:

MRI→learn using that inferred label.

For example, suppose the report says:

“Possible partial ACL tear.”

We might derive:

ACL abnormality → uncertain

Or suppose the report says nothing about the ACL. We might derive:

ACL → unmentioned

These aren't as trustworthy as an expert explicitly reviewing the MRI and assigning an ACL ground-truth label. That is why they are called weak labels, and training from them is called weak supervision.

The distinction is:

Strong supervision: expert directly labels the MRI for the exact task.

Weak supervision: labels come indirectly from a noisy/imperfect source such as a clinical report.

Our project: uses thousands of these weak labels, plus the small strong-label subset to estimate how trustworthy the weak labels are.

And that leads directly to the “noise-aware” part of the title. We are not blindly saying:

report-derived label=ground truth.

Instead, KneeWS asks:

How much should we trust each report-derived label?

For instance, instead of converting everything to:

ACL tear mentioned     → 1
ACL tear not mentioned → 0

KneeWS preserves:

Positive
Explicitly Negative
Uncertain
Unmentioned

and then attempts to produce more realistic probabilistic supervision.

So the title can essentially be read as:

A system for knee MRI analysis that learns mainly from imperfect report-derived labels, while explicitly accounting for the noise and uncertainty in those labels.

That is why “A Noise-Aware Weakly Supervised System…” is technically accurate and directly reflects the core research contribution. deep-research-report
